The research question

Are countries affected by armed conflict and climate stress associated with higher food insecurity? Is there evidence of an additional interaction when the two occur together? Can exposure also travel indirectly through international grain supply chains?

The report evaluates these ideas as five separate questions (RQ1–RQ5), including a descriptive 2027 outlook based on the latest available observations—not a famine prediction.

Scope of the analysis

157Countries represented in the historical FAO FIES panel
5Research questions across direct and indirect exposure
2026Reference snapshot, with three-year FIES windows

The investigation combines FAO FIES (food insecurity), UCDP organized violence, ERA5 climate indicators and FAOSTAT agricultural production and bilateral trade. Coverage differs by question and model; missing data are explicitly flagged rather than silently interpreted as zero.

From raw data to statistical evidence

Official sources→ Validated snapshots→ Country + time harmonisation→ Panel models→ Interactive report

The reproducible Python pipeline downloads and audits data, standardises country identifiers, aggregates annual exposures to FIES three-year windows, checks coverage, and produces model outputs and charts for a Quarto report.

Findings and statistical caution

The descriptive comparison for organized violence shows a difference of approximately 14.87 percentage points in mean food insecurity between exposed and unexposed country-period observations. However, the preferred model with country and period fixed effects estimates an association of approximately +0.58 pp (p = 0.498)—not statistically distinguishable from zero at the 5% threshold.

The analysis also tests climatic indicators, a conflict × drought interaction, and external exposure through trade. Their preferred country-and-period fixed-effects specifications likewise do not establish a robust statistically significant relationship in this dataset.

What matters: large cross-country differences are not automatically causal effects. The work explicitly addresses overlapping observation windows, incomplete geographic coverage, uncertainty and the distinction between measured food insecurity and a predicted risk of famine.

What this project demonstrates

Working beyond a notebook: multi-source data engineering, documented statistical assumptions, panel estimation, hypothesis testing, robustness checks, uncertainty-aware interpretation and an accessible, interactive publication with downloadable processed datasets.
